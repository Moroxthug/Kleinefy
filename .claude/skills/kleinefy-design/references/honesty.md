# Real content and honest product visuals

Adapted from the authentic-product-representation skill in dembrandt-skills, Copyright (c) 2026 Dembrandt, MIT,
https://github.com/dembrandt/dembrandt-skills. Kleinefy changes: "clearly illustrative" figures are not allowed, and
real facts come from the live products.

The quickest way to lose a reader's trust is a visual that is obviously staged. People recognise a fabricated dashboard,
a screenshot full of filler, or a terminal that prints something the real tool never would. The visual is the product,
not a poster of it.

Design with real content. That means real copy, real names and real lengths: the long title that wraps to three lines,
the account with nothing in it. Do not use lorem ipsum. A design that only works with perfect data is not finished.
Empty, loading, error and overflow states belong to the product as much as the happy path does.

When a visual stands in for what a product produces, such as a generated quote, a report or a schedule, reproduce the
real format. Use the same labels, the same ordering and the same truncation, with values taken from a real run. Reuse
the real rendering where you can. A hand-built lookalike drifts away from the product the moment the product changes.
The Kleinefy case studies use the products' own recordings and screenshots in `assets/`, and that is the standard.

Every claim in a visual has to hold. Numbers are real or absent, and Kleinefy does not use "illustrative" figures. Labels
match their content. A demo shows only what the product can do. A "soon" or "pending" label on something that already
ships is a lie, and on a roadmap item it is usually noise. Show what works.

If a real screenshot is impossible, stage the visual honestly. Build it from the real schema and component, keep its
real states and formatting, and keep claims conservative. Then tell the user in the reply what was staged and why.

Fabricated marketing chrome does not belong on a Kleinefy page. That includes invented dashboards and charts, decorative
terminals, gradient text, sparkles, aurora glows, typewriter and word-rotator headlines, faux "thinking" shimmers, and
pill badges that fill space. If an element does not exist in the real product, it does not belong in a visual that
represents the product.

The final test is simple. Could a visitor screenshot the real product and come away with the same impression this visual
gives? If not, change the visual.
